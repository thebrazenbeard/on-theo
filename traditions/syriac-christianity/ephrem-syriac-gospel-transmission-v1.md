## Source layer A — Ephrem's Commentary

Evidence class:
`PRIMARY TEXT` for Ephrem's fourth-century commentary.

Historical setting:
Ephrem (d. 373) worked in the Syriac-speaking world of Nisibis and later
Edessa. His commentary is conventionally identified as a commentary on
Tatian's harmonized Gospel, commonly called the *Diatessaron*.

Scholarly consensus treats the commentary as a major—often premier—witness to
Tatianic Gospel text and arrangement.

Claim ceiling:
`STRONG_FOR_EPHREM_COMMENTARY / INDIRECT_FOR_TATIAN_TEXT`

Guard:
the commentary is not Tatian's Gospel itself. Any Tatianic reading recovered
through Ephrem remains a reconstruction mediated by Ephrem's quotation,
paraphrase, commentary habits, and the later textual transmission of Ephrem.

## Source layer B — Chester Beatty Syriac MS 709

Evidence class:
`MANUSCRIPT WITNESS`

Chester Beatty Syriac MS 709 preserves a substantial Syriac witness to Ephrem's
commentary.

Current catalogue/reference descriptions place the manuscript witness later
than Ephrem, around the late fifth or early sixth century for the relevant
commentary portion.

The manuscript therefore establishes a physical Syriac witness to Ephrem's
commentary, not a fourth-century autograph and not a second-century manuscript
of Tatian.

## Source layer C — Armenian transmission

Modern reference works note that the commentary survives completely only in
Armenian translation, while a large portion—roughly three quarters in standard
reference summaries—survives in Syriac.

Evidence class:
- `MANUSCRIPT WITNESS / TRANSLATION TRADITION` for Armenian transmission;
- `SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION` for reconstruction of the relationship among
  witnesses.

### SYR-EPH-V1-003

Proposition:
The complete shape of Ephrem's commentary is reconstructed using Armenian
transmission in addition to the surviving Syriac material.

Status:
`BOUNDED / STRONG`

Guard:

`ARMENIAN_TRANSLATION != SYRIAC_AUTOGRAPH`

The Armenian witness can preserve lost material while also introducing a
translation layer that must remain visible.

## Source layer D — Ephrem and the separated fourfold Gospel

Matthew R. Crawford's source-critical study argues that Ephrem knew and used
more than Tatian's harmonized Gospel.

The evidence includes:
- awareness of the Synoptic genealogies as distinct material;
- awareness of the Johannine prologue as distinct;
- fourfold-Gospel imagery in Ephrem's corpus;
- references to a "Greek" Gospel text that Crawford interprets as actual Greek
  textual evidence rather than automatically a separated Syriac Gospel.

Crawford concludes that Ephrem's predominant reliance on Tatianic Gospel
material coexisted with awareness and some use of separated Gospel forms.

Evidence class:
`SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION` based on Ephrem's primary corpus.

## Source layer E — Tatian's lost Syriac text

## Historical transition

Scholarship describes the fourth- to fifth-century Syriac Gospel environment as
a period in which Tatianic, separated, Old Syriac, Greek, and eventually
Peshitta-related Gospel forms must be kept distinct.

Crawford emphasizes that Ephrem occupies a transitional moment: Tatian's Gospel
remained highly important while separated Gospel knowledge was already present.
Later fifth-century church policy and textual standardization cannot be
projected backward onto Ephrem's own setting as if the transition occurred in a
single decree or instant.

### SYR-EPH-V1-008

Proposition:
Syriac Gospel transmission should be modeled as layered coexistence and
transition, not one clean replacement event.

Class:
`HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION`

Status:
`BOUNDED / MODERATE_TO_STRONG`

## What this means for reception history

Ephrem is valuable not merely as a passive carrier of a strange Gospel form.

His commentary shows:
- active Syriac exegesis;
- Gospel reception in a linguistic/cultural environment not reducible to Greek
  or Latin Christianity;
- continued authority of a harmonized Gospel text alongside knowledge of
  separated Gospel traditions;
- a textual environment in which "the Gospel" had not yet been reduced to one
  simple transmission story.

This does **not** mean:
- Syriac Christianity was isolated from Greek thought or texts;
- Tatian's Gospel was universally used by all Syriac Christians;
- Peshitta forms instantly displaced all prior Gospel forms;
- Ephrem preserves Tatian verbatim wherever he comments.

## Hostile self-check

Potential overclaim:
"Ephrem used the Diatessaron, therefore fourth-century Syriac Christians did not
know the four canonical Gospels."

Result:
`REJECTED`.

Potential overclaim:
"Chester Beatty 709 is a manuscript of Tatian's Diatessaron."

Result:
`REJECTED`.

It is a manuscript witness to Ephrem's commentary.

Potential overclaim:
"The complete Armenian version gives us Ephrem's original Syriac words."

Result:
`REJECTED`.

It supplies a translated witness to lost commentary material.

Potential overclaim:
"The Diatessaron is extant, so its exact second-century text is secure."

Result:
`REJECTED`.

The original Syriac text is reconstructed indirectly.

Potential overclaim:
"Syriac Gospel transmission was a clean sequence: Diatessaron -> Old Syriac ->
Peshitta."

Result:
`REJECTED`.

The evidence is better modeled as overlapping textual forms and changing
institutional preferences.

## Testament constraint

Book VI may safely say:
- Ephrem wrote in a Syriac Gospel world where Tatian's harmony remained highly
  important;
- his commentary is a major witness to that lost Gospel tradition;
- the surviving Syriac commentary manuscript is later than Ephrem;
- the complete commentary depends on Armenian transmission as well;
- Ephrem also knew separated Gospel material;
- Syriac Gospel transmission involved coexistence and transition rather than a
  single clean replacement.

Book VI should not say:
- we possess Tatian's original Syriac Gospel;
- Ephrem's commentary is a direct second-century witness;
- Tatian's Gospel was the only Gospel known in Ephrem's environment;
- Peshitta standardization happened all at once;
- translation/transmission differences are automatically corruption.
