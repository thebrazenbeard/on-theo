# Shinto — Shrine Material and Institutional Packet V1

Status: MATERIAL / INSTITUTIONAL EVIDENCE CONTROL

## 1. Why material evidence needs its own lane

The existence of a mythic kami in Kojiki or Nihon shoki does not prove:
- a shrine existed at the same time;
- that cult practice matched the literary narrative;
- that the same genealogy controlled local worship.

Likewise, a shrine building does not prove every later myth attached to the shrine was already present when worship began.

Core rule:

`MYTHIC_ATTESTATION`
!=
`SHRINE_CULT_ATTESTATION`.

## 2. Early shrine forms

Kokugakuin's shrine-history synthesis emphasizes that early kami worship did not always require permanent buildings.

Sacred activity could center on:
- natural objects;
- outdoor ritual spaces;
- temporary structures;
- later permanent shrine architecture.

Kasuga is cited as a useful example:
- ritual use of sacred space preceded the later shrine buildings.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9114

### Comparison consequence

The neutral category cannot be:

`TEMPLE_BUILDING = PRESENCE_OF_RELIGION`.

A better question is:

`SACRAL_PRESENCE_MODEL`:
- where/how is sacred presence localized, represented, hosted, invoked, or approached?

Possible answers include:
- place;
- mountain;
- tree;
- rock;
- portable object;
- mirror/sword/jewel;
- shrine building;
- body/person;
- text;
- no fixed locus.

## 3. Shrine architecture is historical

Kokugakuin's architectural history treats shrine building styles as historically developing.

Important controls:
- Ise and Izumo have early architectural traditions;
- some worship remained focused on trees, rocks, and outdoor sites;
- permanent shrine architecture should not be projected uniformly backward;
- periodic rebuilding can preserve architectural style while replacing material fabric.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9696

### Guard

`CURRENT_BUILDING`
!=
`ORIGINAL_MATERIAL_WITNESS`.

`PRESERVED_STYLE`
!=
`PRESERVED_ORIGINAL_TIMBER`.

## 4. Ritsuryō-state system

By the Engishiki period, shrine and kami worship had a substantial state-administrative layer.

Kokugakuin summarizes that the 927 Register of Deities listed:
- 3,132 deities;
- at 2,861 shrines;

receiving court tribute.

It also records a subset of major shrines and regular ritual obligations.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9114

### Comparison consequence

This supports:
- INSTITUTIONALIZATION_MODEL;
- AGENCY_AND_AUTHORITY_MODEL;
- RITUAL_ORDER_MODEL;
- POLITICAL_SACRALIZATION_MODEL as a future candidate.

But:

`COURT_REGISTERED_SHRINE_SYSTEM`
!=
`ALL_LOCAL_JAPANESE_RELIGION`.

## 5. Objects and presence

Kokugakuin's shrine-history account discusses mirrors, swords, jewels, and other objects as loci in which divine presence might be hosted or represented.

This must remain distinct from the later widespread production of anthropomorphic kami images.

Kokugakuin notes that wooden kami images become visible from the late Nara period under Buddhist artistic influence.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9962

### Method consequence

The material representation of sacred presence changes historically.

Therefore:

`KAMI_IMAGE`
is not a timeless prerequisite of kami worship.

## 6. Institutionalization does not equal doctrinal uniformity

By the early Heian period, substantial ritual bureaucracy existed.

That does not mean:
- one theology;
- one mythic genealogy;
- one identical local practice;
- one central church-like institution.

State coordination and local cult plurality can coexist.
