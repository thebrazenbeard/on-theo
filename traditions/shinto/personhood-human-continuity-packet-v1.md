# Shinto — Personhood and Human Continuity Packet V1

Status: DIACHRONIC PERSON/SPIRIT CONTROL / NON-CANONICAL

## Purpose

The current Shinto lane has strong evidence for mythic death, ritual purity, shrine institutions, and later kami-buddha interaction.

This packet asks a narrower comparative question:

> What can the controlled record support about human personhood, spirit, death, and continuity?

The answer is diachronic.

The evidence does **not** support one timeless proposition such as:

`SHINTO = IMMORTAL_SOUL_DOCTRINE`.

Instead, the record contains several historically distinct layers involving `tama`, `mitama`, body/spirit relation, ancestral spirits, dangerous dead, posthumous enshrinement, and later Shinto funerary systems.

## 1. Tama / mitama is broader than "immortal soul"

Kokugakuin's *Encyclopedia of Shinto* summarizes `tama` as an ancient term for spirit/soul, while also noting that the term can refer to spiritual force in nature.

It describes human `tama` as dwelling in the body and contributing energy/personality, and `mitama` as an honorific form.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8644

### Evidence class

This entry is:
- SCHOLARLY SYNTHESIS / LEXICAL INTERPRETATION;
- not itself an ancient primary text.

### Guard

`TAMA`
!=
`ONE_FIXED_WESTERN_SOUL_CATEGORY`.

Because the term can refer to:
- human spirit;
- kami spirit;
- spiritual force associated with nonhuman nature;

it cannot be globally translated as one philosophical substance without locus-specific evidence.

## 2. Chinkon-sai gives stronger documentary evidence for body/spirit relation

The court rite `chinkon-sai` is a better-controlled historical anchor.

The *Ryō no gige*, an official commentary on the Yōrō legal code completed in 833, describes the rite as recalling/pacifying a spirit that is trying to depart from the body.

The rite served to strengthen the emperor's spirit before major court rituals.

Sources:
- https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9291
- https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/k/img_auth.php/9/98/Como_2009.pdf

### Comparison consequence

At the level of early court ritual ideology:

`HUMAN_BODY`
and
`TAMA/MITAMA`

can be treated as distinguishable enough that the spirit's departure, return, stabilization, or strengthening is ritually meaningful.

This supports:

`PERSON_OR_SELF_MODEL = BODY_PLUS_RITUALLY_MANAGED_SPIRIT_COMPONENT`

for a bounded court layer.

### Guard

The emperor-centered court rite does not establish:
- one universal Japanese anthropology;
- one philosophical theory of an immortal soul;
- one doctrine shared unchanged by all classes and periods.

## 3. Tamashizume / tamafuri preserves multiple ritual interpretations

Kokugakuin summarizes:
- `tamashizume` / `mitamashizume`: pacifying and settling spirit in the body;
- `tamafuri` / `mitamafuri`: reinvigorating spirit or beckoning spirit into the body.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8752

This strengthens the body/spirit distinction at the level of ritual discourse.

But even here, later scholarly explanations of the rite differ.

### Guard

`RITUAL_MANIPULABILITY_OF_TAMA`
does not by itself establish:
- Cartesian substance dualism;
- reincarnation;
- resurrection;
- a complete postmortem ontology.

## 4. Classical death narratives remain literary, not universal anthropology

The Kojiki/Nihon shoki Yomi cycle already supports:
- death;
- separation;
- continuing identity of mythic figures;
- pollution associated with death.

A second classical-text locator is the Nihon shoki account of Uji-no-Waki-iratsuko, where a dead human prince is represented as temporarily returning to life when called by his brother.

Kokugakuin's mythology research uses this episode as evidence relevant to historical interpretations of `tamafuri`.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8621

### Evidence ceiling

The exact Nihon shoki passage remains a primary-text binding frontier in this lane.

Current status:

`PRIMARY_TEXT_LOCATOR_IDENTIFIED / EXACT_EDITION_LINE_BINDING_PENDING`.

Therefore the episode may support:
- HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION of continuity ideas;
- literary possibility of recoverable life/spirit;

but it does not yet become a fully bound general Shinto anthropology proposition.

## 5. Ancient imperial ancestor spirits are a different evidence layer

Kokugakuin's spirit-history synthesis notes:
- ancient rites directed to spirits of deceased emperors;
- `Shoku Nihongi` imperial decrees referring to the activity/effects of imperial ancestral spirits.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8756

### Evidence status

This is a secondary locator to:
- documentary/court ancestor-spirit evidence;
- exact `Shoku Nihongi` primary passages still requiring direct binding.

### Comparison consequence

The current record supports at least a bounded proposition that:

`DECEASED_HUMAN_IDENTITY_OR_AGENCY_CAN_REMAIN_RITUALLY_RELEVANT`

in court/ancestral contexts.

It does **not** yet establish one universal ancestor doctrine.

## 6. Heian goryō shows postmortem human agency more directly

The first written reference to a `goryō-e` is associated with the Shinsen'en rite of 863.

The rite was performed to pacify dangerous or vengeful spirits associated with calamity and epidemic disease.

Sources:
- https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9021
- https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8945

This is historically later than the Kiki compilations and is already a Buddhist-kami combinatory environment.

### Comparison consequence

By the early Heian period the documentary record clearly allows:

`DEAD_HUMAN_SPIRIT -> CONTINUING_SOCIAL_CAUSAL_AGENT`

within a religious/ritual model.

### Guard

`GORYO_863`
!=
`PRIMORDIAL_SHINTO_SOUL_DOCTRINE`.

The ritual includes Buddhist components and belongs to a contact-transformed religious environment.

## 7. Sugawara Michizane demonstrates posthumous status transformation

The cult of Sugawara no Michizane is a particularly useful diachronic case.

Kokugakuin's Tenjin history records:
- a mausoleum near Dazaifu by 905;
- development of Michizane's spirit as a dangerous `goryō`;
- later official recognition/deification as Tenjin;
- substantial Buddhist and other religious reinterpretation.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8880

### Comparison consequence

A deceased human can be represented through changing posthumous statuses:

`HUMAN_PERSON`
->
`DEAD_SPIRIT`
->
`GORYO`
->
`ENSHRINED_KAMI / TENJIN`.

This is strong evidence for:

`POSTHUMOUS_STATUS_TRANSFORMATION`

as a historical process.

It is not evidence that every human automatically becomes a kami.

## 8. Human-kami worship expands historically

Kokugakuin's `ikigami` and spirit-history entries describe:
- veneration of exceptional humans;
- posthumous enshrinement of humans;
- later Yoshida Shinto institutionalization of human-as-kami practices;
- early-modern expansion of such cults.

Sources:
- https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9948
- https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8756

The historical process matters more than the generic label.

### Guard

`HUMAN_CAN_BECOME_ENSHRINED_AS_KAMI`
!=
`ALL_HUMANS_ARE_KAMI_AFTER_DEATH`.

## 9. Ancestor doctrine must be period-controlled

Modern and folk-religious descriptions of `sorei` often describe:
- a dead person's spirit receiving memorial rites;
- gradual loss or transformation of individuality in some models;
- possible elevation into an ancestral/tutelary spirit.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8647

But Kokugakuin explicitly attributes important formulations here to modern folklorists such as Yanagita Kunio.

Likewise, broad ancestor rites show:
- Buddhist influence;
- Confucian influence;
- court and household variation;
- major early-modern and modern developments.

Sources:
- https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9019
- https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9675

### Guard

`YANAGITA_ANCESTRAL_SPIRIT_MODEL`
!=
`DIRECT_ANCIENT_SHINTO_PRIMARY_TEXT_FACT`.

## 10. Modern Shinto funerals are especially late evidence

Formal Shinto funerary manuals expanded in the late Edo period.

An official Shinto funeral procedure was promulgated in 1872, and later Shrine Shinto developed standardized modern funeral practices.

Source:
https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8905

These rites can include:
- transfer of the deceased spirit to a spirit vessel;
- household memorial presence;
- food offerings;
- later joining with family ancestral spirits.

### Evidence class

This is valuable evidence for:

`MODERN_SHINTO_CONTINUITY_MODEL`.

It is **not** direct evidence for:
- Kofun-period funerary belief;
- Kojiki-era commoner practice;
- one unchanged indigenous death system.

## 11. Buddhist dominance of Japanese death ritual is part of the historical record

Modern scholarship on Japanese death and afterlife emphasizes that Buddhism dominated Japanese death ritual for more than a millennium while assimilating and reshaping other practices and concepts.

Useful orientation:
- Jacqueline Stone and Mariko Walter, *Death and the Afterlife in Japanese Buddhism*;
- John Breen and Mark Teeuwen, *Shinto in History: Ways of the Kami*.

The methodological consequence is straightforward:

`JAPANESE_DEATH_PRACTICE`
cannot be partitioned into an untouched "Shinto" substrate plus later foreign additions without source-specific historical analysis.

## 12. Person/self comparison result

The current Shinto lane can now admit:

### PERSON_OR_SELF_MODEL
**PARTIAL_BOUNDED**

Supported:
- human `tama/mitama` as a conceptual/ritual component associated with the embodied person;
- ritual concern with spirit departure, return, stabilization, or strengthening;
- persistence of named human identity in some postmortem religious histories.

Not supported:
- one philosophical definition of the self;
- one universal human soul substance;
- one timeless doctrine across classical, medieval, early-modern, and modern Shinto.

Neutral summary:

`EMBODIED_PERSON_WITH_RITUALLY_DISTINGUISHABLE_SPIRIT_COMPONENT / DIAchRONIC_VARIATION`.
