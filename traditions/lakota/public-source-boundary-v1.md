# Lakota Public-Source and Sacred-Knowledge Boundary V1

Status: ACTIVE RESEARCH CONTROL / NON-CANONICAL

## Purpose

This lane reconstructs Lakota religious, ceremonial, relational, and sacred-land
traditions from public sources while refusing to treat public accessibility as
permission to reproduce restricted ceremonial knowledge.

Core rule:

PUBLICLY_ACCESSIBLE != ETHICALLY_UNRESTRICTED.

A second core rule:

LAKOTA != GENERIC_NATIVE_AMERICAN_RELIGION.

The lane is nation/community specific and diachronic.

## 1. Source hierarchy

### Tier A — living Lakota/Dakota community and tribal institutions

Preferred first:
- tribal government publications;
- tribal legal codes;
- tribally controlled colleges and educational programs;
- public statements by named Lakota leaders or knowledge keepers;
- community cultural programs.

Current high-value examples:
- Oglala Lakota College;
- Sitting Bull College;
- Rosebud Sioux Tribe / Sicangu Lakota Nation;
- Standing Rock Sioux Tribe.

Evidence use:
DOCUMENTARY_PRACTICE + MODERN_TRADITION + COMMUNITY_SELF_DESCRIPTION.

These sources establish contemporary public formulations and living institutional
practice.

They do not automatically establish unchanged precontact antiquity.

### Tier B — public institutional historical context

Examples:
- National Park Service;
- Library of Congress;
- federal statute;
- state historic/cultural-resource documentation.

Evidence use:
DOCUMENTARY_PRACTICE + HISTORICAL_RECONSTRUCTION.

Use primarily for:
- suppression history;
- legal protection;
- sacred-site context;
- publicly documented historical continuity.

These sources do not override Lakota community self-description.

### Tier C — Lakota testimony mediated through non-Lakota recorder/editor

Key example:
Black Elk / Joseph Epes Brown, The Sacred Pipe.

Classification:
LATER_TRADITION + COMMENTARY + HISTORICAL_ETHNOGRAPHIC_RECORD.

Use for:
- what Black Elk publicly taught Brown;
- the mid-twentieth-century record of an Oglala holy man's account.

Do not treat as:
- one universal Lakota catechism;
- verbatim unmediated community consensus;
- proof that every ceremony was identical across bands or time.

Black Elk Speaks requires an even stronger editorial control because John
Neihardt substantially shaped the literary presentation and the scholarly
history of that editing is contested.

### Tier D — historical ethnography

Older ethnography can preserve valuable language, accounts, and participant
testimony.

It can also contain:
- outsider categories;
- colonial bias;
- Christian theological translations;
- procedural disclosure that living communities may not regard as appropriate
  for unrestricted reuse.

Therefore:

HISTORICAL_ETHNOGRAPHY != TRANSPARENT_LAKOTA_SELF_DESCRIPTION.

### Tier E — scholarly interpretation

Use for:
- diachronic reconstruction;
- lexical analysis;
- ontology/personhood models;
- source criticism;
- contact history.

Scholarly interpretation never outranks explicit community correction merely
because it is academically published.

## 2. Restricted knowledge rule

Do not reproduce:
- closed ceremonial instructions;
- initiatory knowledge;
- sacred bundle contents beyond what communities publicly authorize;
- private prayers or songs not clearly intended for public circulation;
- restricted sacred-site details;
- knowledge identified by a community/source as not for outsiders.

Even when an older ethnographer printed such material, ON_THEO may cite the
existence/history of the source without reproducing operational detail.

## 3. Ceremony handling

Public sources may establish:
- that a ceremony exists;
- its public name;
- broad social/religious purpose;
- publicly stated relational or ethical meaning;
- historical suppression or continuity.

That does not automatically authorize:
- procedural reconstruction;
- step-by-step performance instructions;
- publication of private songs/prayers;
- generalized claims across all Lakota communities.

## 4. Translation controls

English glosses require source tagging.

Especially:

WAKAN / WAKAN TANKA
may be rendered in public sources as:
- sacred;
- mysterious;
- Great Mystery;
- Great Spirit;
- Creator;
- related formulations.

Therefore:

ONE_ENGLISH_GLOSS != ONE_EXHAUSTIVE_LAKOTA_THEOLOGY.

Contemporary tribal/legal English language can use "Creator" or "supreme being"
without proving that all historical Lakota conceptions reduce to Christian-style
monotheism.

## 5. Community diversity control

Lakota communities are not one timeless ritual unit.

Track where possible:
- Oglala;
- Sicangu / Brule;
- Hunkpapa;
- Sihasapa;
- Miniconjou;
- Oohenumpa / Two Kettle;
- Itazipco / Sans Arc;
- reservation/community context;
- historical period;
- source speaker.

Standing Rock sources can be Lakota/Dakota institutional sources and must not be
silently relabeled as exclusively Lakota where the source speaks more broadly.

## 6. Colonial/contact layer

Missionary, federal, boarding-school, reservation, and legal histories are part
of the religious record because they changed:
- ceremony visibility;
- public/private practice;
- language transmission;
- sacred-site access;
- Christian/traditional interaction;
- institutions of cultural transmission.

Colonial suppression does not imply tradition disappeared.

Modern public practice does not prove an unchanged precontact form.

## 7. Comparison hold

No Lakota proposition enters the existing cross-tradition comparison matrix
until:
- internal source mapping is adequate;
- source speaker/community is identified where possible;
- historical/modern layers are separated;
- restricted-knowledge risk is reviewed;
- the specific neutral axis is source-admitted.
