# Yavanajātaka Dating and Textual-Reconstruction Dispute V1

Status: PRIMARY SCHOLARLY DISPUTE / DATE UNRESOLVED

## Why this matters

The Yavanajātaka has often been used as a fixed chronological anchor for the
arrival of Hellenistic horoscopy in India.

That use is no longer methodologically safe without qualification.

## Pingree 1978 reconstruction

David Pingree's edition and translation proposed:

- an earlier prose work associated with Yavaneśvara in 149/150 CE;
- a Sanskrit versification by Sphujidhvaja in 269/270 CE;
- an originally Greek/Alexandrian source behind the prose;
- mathematical material of ultimately Babylonian origin;
- an early zero/place-value reading in the final chapter.

Mak 2013 summarizes these claims before re-examining them.

Source:
https://hssa-journal.org/index.php/hssa/article/view/7

Classification:
SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION / EDITORIAL_RECONSTRUCTION.

## Earlier criticism

Before Mak's new-manuscript study, important elements of Pingree's edition had
already been challenged by scholars including:
- K. S. Shukla;
- Harry Falk.

The disputed points included textual readings and chronological interpretation.

## New manuscript evidence

Bill Mak's 2013 study uses additional evidence including an unreported Nepalese
paper manuscript from the Nepal-German Manuscript Preservation Project.

The new witness matters because several of Pingree's decisive chronological
readings depended on emendation of damaged or ambiguous manuscript text.

## Date result

Mak's conclusion is stronger than “the date might be a little different.”

He argues that the conventional:
- 149/150 CE prose-composition date;
- 269/270 CE versification date

must be discarded as established dates.

Direct article text:
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/292140116_The_date_and_nature_of_Sphujidhvaja's_Yavanajataka_reconsidered_in_the_light_of_some_newly_discovered_materials

Mak's bounded conclusion is:

- the work is an early Indian jyotiṣa text incorporating Greek astrology and astronomy;
- the exact date remains uncertain;
- it is after 22 CE;
- it could be as late as the early seventh century, when quotations become externally attested.

Therefore the branch date state is:

YAVANAJATAKA_DATE = UNRESOLVED_WITH_BOUNDS

not:

YAVANAJATAKA_DATE = 269_270_CE.

## No secure lost Greek prose original

Mak states that there is no evidence establishing the specific lost Greek prose
text from which Pingree said Sphujidhvaja versified.

This does not erase Greek influence.

It separates:

GREEK_TECHNICAL_INFLUENCE
from
SPECIFIC_LOST_GREEK_PROSE_ARCHETYPE.

## Greek technical vocabulary remains significant

Mak still identifies conspicuous Greek parallels/loans in the first 78 chapters,
including terms associated with:
- ascendant / horā;
- decan / drekkāṇa;
- minute / liptā.

He also emphasizes Indian material:
- karma;
- Ayurveda;
- Hindu deities;
- technical elements not attested in extant Greek sources.

The better model is therefore synthesis/amalgamation, not a simple translation
pipeline.

## Yavaneśvara and Sphujidhvaja identity problem

Pingree treated them as two historical individuals tied to two dates.

Mak notes that:
- Bhāskara appears to combine the names;
- later citations frequently attribute the work to Yavaneśvara;
- Yavaneśvara may function as a title/general attribution rather than a uniquely
  recoverable person.

Current state:
UNRESOLVED.

## Chapter structure problem

Mak also challenges Pingree's chapter reconstruction.

For example:
- the title “horāvidhiḥ” is an editorial emendation;
- one manuscript groups material differently;
- some chapter titles were conjectured rather than manuscript-attested.

This means:

EDITORIAL_CHAPTER_NUMBER != AUTOMATIC_PRIMARY_TEXT_STRUCTURE.

## Zero claim

Mak rejects the specific famous Yavanajātaka evidence for:
- a zero placeholder;
- the bhūtasaṃkhyā readings used in Pingree's chronology.

Guard:

REFUTED_ZERO_READING_IN_THIS_TEXT != ZERO_WAS_NOT_INVENTED_IN_INDIA.

It only removes this text as secure evidence for that particular claim.

## Research consequence

Every on-theo claim that currently says:

“Greek astrology entered India by 149/150 CE via Yavaneśvara”

must be rewritten more cautiously.

Safer statement:

Hellenistic/Greek astrological elements entered and were transformed within
Indian jyotiṣa in the early centuries CE, but the Yavanajātaka cannot presently
serve as a securely dated 149/150 or 269/270 CE anchor.
