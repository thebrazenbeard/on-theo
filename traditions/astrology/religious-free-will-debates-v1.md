## Purpose

Religious and philosophical responses to astrology cannot be reduced to:

RELIGION_OPPOSED_ASTROLOGY.

Historically, thinkers distinguish among:
- celestial influence;
- celestial prediction;
- fatalism;
- divination;
- human free will;
- divine providence;
- demons/spirits;
- legitimate astronomy.

This file records those distinctions.

## Greco-Roman: Ptolemy

Ptolemy presents astrology as natural prognostication rather than irrevocable personal fate.

He holds that:
- celestial motions are regular;
- terrestrial matter is variable;
- multiple causes interact;
- weaker tendencies can be overcome by stronger causes.

Source:
Ptolemy, Tetrabiblos I.1–3
https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/ptolemy/tetrabiblos/1a*.html

Classification:
PRIMARY_TEXT.

## Early Christian critique

Pseudo-Clementine Recognitions, Book X, preserves a Christian argument that human free choice disrupts astrological prediction.

Text:
https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/080410.htm

The argument is not simply that celestial bodies do not exist or move predictably. It attacks the claim that their configurations determine human acts.

## Augustine tradition

Augustinian critique distinguishes bodily/natural effects from determination of the rational will.

Aquinas later transmits and develops this distinction.

## Thomas Aquinas

Aquinas allows celestial bodies to affect bodily conditions and therefore indirectly incline sensual appetites, while denying that stars directly determine intellect and will.

Relevant text:
Summa Theologiae, II-II, material on divination and celestial influence.
Accessible Latin/English:
https://aquinas.cc/

A Notre Dame overview summarizes the distinction:
- stars can influence bodily/emotional conditions;
- free rational action is not necessitated;
- natural phenomena genuinely caused by celestial bodies are a different category from predicting contingent free acts.

Source:
https://churchlifejournal.nd.edu/articles/a-very-short-introduction-to-aquinas/

Control:

AQUINAS_ACCEPTS_CELESTIAL_NATURAL_EFFECTS != AQUINAS_ENDORSES_NATAL_FATALISM.

## Islamic astral practice

Astrology was a major learned practice in Abbasid and later Islamic societies.

Abū Maʿshar drew on Greek, Indian, Sasanian, Syrian, and Arabic sources.

Source:
https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/abu-masar-jafar-b/

Māshāʾallāh and Nawbakht were associated with selecting an astrologically appropriate foundation time for Baghdad in 762.

Source:
https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/abu-sahl-b/

This is strong evidence that elite Islamic political culture could use astrology.

It does not mean Islamic theology uniformly approved it.

## Ibn Sīnā / Avicenna

Ibn Sīnā wrote a treatise explicitly refuting astrology:
Risāla fī ibṭāl aḥkām al-nujūm / Treatise on the Refutation of Astrology.

Max Planck Institute Islamic Scientific Manuscripts Initiative:
https://ismi.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/biography/Ibn_Sina_BEA.htm

Important distinction:
Ibn Sīnā's cosmology can permit celestial influence while rejecting astrologers' capacity to derive precise judgments from it.

This means:

CELESTIAL_CAUSATION_ACCEPTED
does not entail
HOROSCOPIC_PREDICTION_ACCEPTED.

## al-Ghazālī / Ashʿarite causality

Al-Ghazālī emphasizes God's causal sovereignty.

Stanford Encyclopedia:
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/al-ghazali/

His framework sharply complicates any doctrine in which celestial bodies function as autonomous causal powers.

Research caution:
Do not label al-Ghazālī simply “anti-causal.” His discussions of causation, occasionalism, and secondary causes require proposition-level reading.

## Islamic divination boundary

Islamic legal/theological traditions often distinguish legitimate astronomical knowledge from claims to forbidden knowledge of the future.

Encyclopaedia Iranica's divination overview notes traditions in which attempting to obtain future knowledge reserved for God is condemned.

Source:
https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/divination/

This boundary must be analyzed separately from whether stars were believed to have natural effects.

## Medieval Jewish astrology: Abraham ibn Ezra

Abraham ibn Ezra treated astrology as intellectually central and became a major transmitter of Arabic astral science into Hebrew and Latin contexts.

Stanford Encyclopedia:
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-ezra/

Cambridge:
https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/arabic-sciences-and-philosophy/article/abs/astronomy-and-astrology-in-the-works-of-abraham-ibn-ezra/28B26742885E93988237BE9EB6BD79DA

His system attempts to reconcile strong celestial governance of the material world with divine providence and a path for human/spiritual escape from ordinary astral determination.

## Maimonides

Maimonides rejected astrology strongly.

His 1194 letter to the sages of Provence/Marseilles treats astrology as lacking valid epistemic foundation and as conflicting with free choice and proper theology.

Scholarly edition/discussion:
Ralph Lerner, “Maimonides' Letter on Astrology,” History of Religions 8.2 (1968):
https://doi.org/10.1086/462581

Overview:
https://ccel.org/ccel/maimonides/guide/guide.iv.i.html

This provides an internal Jewish disagreement:

IBN_EZRA_POSITIVE_ASTROLOGY != MAIMONIDES_REJECTION.

## Why this matters to on-theo

The recurring conceptual problem is:

If the heavens influence human beings, what remains of agency?

Historical solutions include:
- celestial signs rather than causes;
- bodily influence but rational freedom;
- inclinations rather than necessities;
- divine override/providence;
- causal complexity preventing exact prediction;
- total rejection of astrological judgment.

This maps cleanly onto the simulation branch without claiming identity.

A simulated agent could likewise have:
- initialization priors;
- runtime influences;
- stochastic outcomes;
