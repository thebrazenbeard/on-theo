## Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa

The Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa is a short Sanskrit astronomical/calendrical work attributed to Lagadha and preserved in two recensions associated with the Ṛgveda and Yajurveda.

Bibliothèque nationale de France authority record:
https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb162151450

The BnF record conservatively gives the work an indeterminate date range and records the two recensions.

The dating problem is real.

Astronomical reconstructions have proposed substantially earlier epochs based on solstitial/nakṣatra data, while other scholars distinguish:
- the epoch encoded by astronomical parameters;
- the date of the surviving textual composition/redaction.

A 2021 Indian Journal of History of Science paper demonstrates that a proposed unique 1800 BCE epoch cannot be established from one modern archaeoastronomical criterion.

Source:
https://insa.nic.in/writereaddata/UpLoadedFiles/IJHS/14-19.pdf
DOI: 10.1007/s43539-021-00001-5

## Nakṣatras

Nakṣatra systems divide the lunar/stellar path into stations.

Research questions:
- 27 versus 28 station systems;
- earliest textual attestations;
- ritual/calendar role;
- stellar referents;
- relation to later horoscopy;
- relationship, if any, to Chinese lodge systems.

Similarity in count is insufficient to prove transmission.

## Hellenistic contact and horā literature

Classical Indian horoscopic astrology contains major structures with strong Hellenistic parallels.

These include, depending on text and period:
- twelve rāśis;
- houses/bhāvas;
- planetary rulership;
- aspects;
- horā terminology;
- decanic/drekkāṇa-type divisions;
- natal judgment.

The transmission model must still avoid two opposite errors:

ERROR A:
“All Indian astrology is imported Greek astrology.”

ERROR B:
“Classical Indian horoscopy is untouched Vedic continuity.”

The historical system is composite.

## Yavanajātaka

The Yavanajātaka is central to the Greco-Indian transmission problem.

David Pingree’s influential 1978 reconstruction proposed:
- Greek prose original translated by Yavaneśvara around 149/150 CE;
- Sanskrit versification by Sphujidhvaja in 269/270 CE.

Bill Mak's 2013 reassessment uses newly available manuscript evidence to challenge key readings behind that chronology.

Source:
Bill M. Mak, “The Date and Nature of Sphujidhvaja’s Yavanajātaka Reconsidered...”
https://doi.org/10.18732/H2RP4T

Mak identifies flaws already raised by earlier scholars and reopens:
- dating;
- textual reconstruction;
- identity of layers;
- alleged earliest zero reference.

## Varāhamihira

Varāhamihira, sixth century CE, is a major synthesis point.

### Bṛhat Jātaka

The Bṛhat Jātaka is a natal/horoscopic text.

Accessible Sanskrit/English edition:
https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-jataka-by-varahamihira-sanskrit-english

Examples of technical content include:
- signs/rāśis;
- houses;
- planetary conditions;
- navāṃśas and other divisions;
- longevity;
- daśā-like timing material;
- reconstruction of lost horoscopes.

A passage explicitly names Yavana among earlier authorities, which is relevant to cross-cultural intellectual provenance.

Source example:
https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-jataka-by-varahamihira-sanskrit-english/d/doc1501673.html

### Bṛhat Saṃhitā

The Bṛhat Saṃhitā demonstrates the breadth of saṃhitā-style jyotiṣa.

Accessible translation:
https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-samhita

Chapter 2 describes the competent jyotiṣaka as versed in:
- saṃhitā;
- astronomy;
- horoscopy.

This is useful evidence against reducing jyotiṣa to “birth charts.”

The work spans astral, calendrical, omenological, meteorological, agricultural, ritual, architectural, and other knowledge.

A chapter on planetary conjunctions explicitly notes prediction by astronomical calculation.

Source:
https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-samhita/d/doc228918.html

## Technical categories requiring separate provenance

### Gaṇita
Mathematical/astronomical calculation.

### Saṃhitā
Broad astral/omenological and worldly prognostication.

### Horā
Horoscopic/natal and related judgment traditions.

These boundaries vary by author and period but are analytically useful.

## Later transmission into India

Indian astral science also later receives Persian/Arabic material.

Tājika traditions must therefore be treated as another historical transmission layer, not as proof that every Indian astrological technique is Vedic or Hellenistic.

Open work:
- Tājika source chronology;
- Perso-Arabic aspects and annual revolution techniques;
- Sanskritization of imported terminology.

## Mechanism diversity

Indian sources may frame astral relations through combinations of:
- omen;
- karma;
- deity/ritual;
- cosmic correspondence;
- natural influence;
- calendrical timing.

Do not impose one causal theory on all jyotiṣa.
