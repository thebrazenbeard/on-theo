## Passage results — retain the speaker

### XVII.85–105: what loves, and how it errs

**PRIMARY TEXT / VIRGIL'S DISCOURSE:** The fourth terrace remedies deficient love of the good; no creator/creature is without love. The Italian distinguishes natural love (presented as error-free) from love 'of the mind' or elective love, which may err in its *object* or through too much/too little vigor. Love is presented as source of both virtuous conduct and punishable action. The reported English gloss is not a proof of equivalent medieval doctrinal terms.

**Interpretive ceiling:** A textual moral architecture of ordered desire. It is too coarse to say that all love is sinful, or that Dante endorses a modern psychological model. Virgil, a guide in a Christian epic, is both privileged and limited; elsewhere Dante places revealed guidance beyond his competence.

### XVII.106–139: why seven terraces are not seven moral dimensions

**PRIMARY TEXT:** Virgil divides misdirected love toward a neighbor (three lower terraces), deficient pursuit of right good (one middle terrace) and excessive attachment to secondary goods (three upper terraces). These groupings are narrative/moral categories; their sevenfold ordering is not defined as seven independent quantities measured simultaneously.

**INFERENCE:** One may construct a modern seven-mode inventory of disordered attachment, but a dimensional reading changes the explanatory language. Mathematical independence, orthogonality, distance metric and predictive rules are absent from this moral discourse.

### XVIII.49–75: desire and assent

**PRIMARY TEXT / VIRGIL:** Innate knowledge of first principles and first appetites are not immediately morally praiseworthy or blameworthy; the poem puts moral accountability at the threshold of deliberative assent. Even under an assumption that love arises necessarily, the person has power to hold or restrain it. Virgil defers deeper exposition to Beatrice.

A short load-bearing Italian phrase, XVIII.62–63: "la virtù che consiglia, / e de l'assenso de' tener la soglia." (The faculty that counsels and guards assent.) Italian in Petrocchi as digitally displayed; paraphrase rather than presenting this as an independently checked translation.

**Interpretive ceiling:** Dante represents elective evaluation as morally significant despite involuntary promptings. It does not license the claim that desire is freely produced at will, that habit does not matter, or that Dante is describing contemporary clinical therapy.

### XXVII.127–142: outcome and limitation

**PRIMARY TEXT / VIRGIL:** Virgil relinquishes guidance after the pilgrim traverses Hell and Purgatory. At verse 140 he calls Dante's arbitrium "libero, dritto e sano" (free, upright and sound). This is the poem's unusually explicit marker of reordered agency. Verses 141–142 amplify self-governance.

**COUNTERWEIGHT:** This occurs *before* the encounters with Matelda and Beatrice in the Earthly Paradise and before Paradiso. The apparent completion of moral self-rule is not identical to total theological or intellectual fulfillment.

## Comparison: same lived world, different internal states?

**B / MODERN INTERPRETATION:** In lived human experience, a transition from fixation through reform to harmonized choice can serve as an allegory of earthly existence. Its plausibility rests on thematic analogy, not on a Dante-authored proposition that the inhabited Earth is postmortem Purgatory.

**A / TEXTUAL GEOGRAPHY:** Dante's literal fictional topography distinguishes inhabited Earth, infernal cavity, antipodal mountain, Earthly Paradise and celestial realms, with dead souls subject to purgation; the living pilgrim is the exceptional visitor. Thus "Earth = literal Purgatory" exceeds the poem's own distinctions even though the mountain is geographically placed on Earth.

**PHYSICS / FALSIFIER:** Seven terraces may be paired numerically with seven spatial compactification dimensions in an 11-dimensional M-theory framing (4+7). This does not create a valid physical identification. There is no defined correspondence between a moral terrace and a coordinate direction, and selecting seven terraces while ignoring nine infernal circles or nine heavens is post hoc.

## First comparative challenge: late commentary vs poem

Francesco da Buti's late-fourteenth-century commentary on XVII explicitly parses natural and elective love and explains the three error types. This corroborates **a medieval interpretation** of these verses, not Dante's personal intention or the earliest reception:
https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/Commedia_%28Buti%29/Purgatorio/Canto_XVII

Earlier commentary comparison (Jacopo Alighieri, Jacopo della Lana, Ottimo) remains **NOT DONE**; do not falsely claim a reception consensus. Dartmouth chronological index:
