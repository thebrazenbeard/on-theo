## Research question

What can the manuscript and linguistic evidence actually tell us about the age and internal structure of the early Welsh Myrddin corpus, independently of later assumptions that all relevant poems describe one historical sixth-century individual?

## Current scholarly control surface

The strongest current working edition is the AHRC-funded **Welsh Merlin Poetry Project / Prosiect Barddoniaeth Myrddin**, a collaboration between Cardiff University, the University of Wales Centre for Advanced Welsh and Celtic Studies, and Swansea University.

Project:
https://merlinpoetry.wales/

Staff and editorial assignments:
https://www.merlinpoetry.wales/about/

Current project scale reported by Cardiff (2025):
- 102 poems edited;
- 4,450 lines;
- 519 manuscript witnesses;
- seven major early poems in medieval manuscripts plus 95 later poems.

Source:
https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/news/view/2899063-discovering-the-true-origins-of-merlin

This critical edition materially supersedes any workflow that treats a single Black Book transcription as the entire textual tradition.

## Evidence firewall

- MANUSCRIPT DATE != COMPOSITION DATE
- EARLY LANGUAGE != SIXTH-CENTURY AUTHORSHIP
- FIRST-PERSON VOICE != AUTOBIOGRAPHY
- PROPHETIC FULFILMENT != FOREKNOWLEDGE
- LATER ATTRIBUTION TO MYRDDIN != ORIGINAL AUTHORSHIP
- ONE MANUSCRIPT'S OMISSION != ORIGINAL ABSENCE
- TEXTUAL COHERENCE != HISTORICITY
- CORPUS ASSOCIATION != COMMON AUTHOR

## Finding 1 — The Black Book Afallennau is excerpted, not complete

The current Welsh Merlin Poetry Project reports that comparison across witnesses demonstrates that the Black Book of Carmarthen does **not** preserve the full text of `Yr Afallennau`; it preserves extracts.

Source:
Cardiff University, "Myrddin Poetry Project – what treasures have been unearthed?"
https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/welsh/news/features/prosiect-barddoniaeth-myrddin-what-treasures-have-been-unearthed

Implication:
Absence of a motif, name, or stanza from Peniarth 1 cannot automatically be projected backward as absence from an earlier form of the poem. Reconstruction must use the full witness tradition.

State: SURVIVES.

## Finding 2 — Afallennau / Oianau speakers are not securely named Myrddin in the Black Book

The National Library of Wales states that Myrddin is named only in `Ymddiddan Myrddin a Thaliesin` among the three poems definitely connected to the legend; `Yr Afallennau` and `Yr Oianau` instead present an unnamed forest-dweller, although Caerfyrddin appears once in `Yr Oianau`.

Source:
https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/the-black-book-of-carmarthen

Ben Guy explicitly notes that the original association of the name Myrddin with `Y Bedwenni`, `Yr Afallennau`, and `Yr Oianau` has been disputed, citing Oliver Padel 2006.

Source:
Guy 2024, p. 4 n.7:
https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/156478/1/The%20distribution%20of%203.%20pl.%20pres.%20ind.%20-ynt%20ORCA%20VERSION.pdf

Implication:
The forest-prophet tradition and the named Myrddin prophetic authority must be analytically separable until their linkage is independently established.

State: SURVIVES.

## Finding 3 — Ymddiddan contains a named Myrddin layer but is itself composite in subject

A. O. H. Jarman's edition, translated in a Utrecht thesis under Peter Schrijver, divides `Ymddiddan Myrddin a Thaliesin` into:
- lines 1–22: Maelgwn Gwynedd / Dyfed conflict;
- lines 23–38: Arfderydd prophetic material.

The final stanza explicitly identifies the speaker as Myrddin in relation to Taliesin ("I, Myrddin, after Taliesin..." in the translation).

Source:
https://studylib.net/doc/7432132/the-conversation-of-myrddin-and-taliesin

Jarman estimated composition around the end of the eleventh century. This date remains an editorial reconstruction, not a manuscript date.

State: NAMED MYRDDIN TRADITION EXISTS PRE-BLACK-BOOK IF JARMAN'S DATING HOLDS; EXACT STRATIGRAPHY UNRESOLVED.

## Finding 4 — Cyfoesi is now better treated as a coherent composition, not accretion

The Welsh Merlin Poetry Project reports that its new critical editing supports `Cyfoesi Myrddin a Gwenddydd ei Chwaer` as a unified and coherent composition centred on Myrddin, Gwenddydd, death, and the end of the world.

Source:
https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/welsh/news/features/prosiect-barddoniaeth-myrddin-what-treasures-have-been-unearthed

Ben Guy's independent linguistic dating work says the editing process points to composition around the **beginning of the thirteenth century**. Two 3rd-person plural present indicative forms in -ynt fit this dating picture.

Source:
Guy 2024, pp. 12–13:
https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/156478/1/The%20distribution%20of%203.%20pl.%20pres.%20ind.%20-ynt%20ORCA%20VERSION.pdf

Implication:
Older claims of a ninth-/tenth-century Cyfoesi core should not be used as default chronology.

State: SURVIVES_NARROWED — current best working date early 13th century, pending full critical-edition commentary.

## Finding 5 — Peirian Faban likely belongs to the 12th century

Guy finds archaic verbal endings suggesting the poem is unlikely to postdate the early thirteenth century. Its reference to the `freinc` ("French") makes a date after the Norman Conquest of 1066 likely, yielding an approximate twelfth-century composition.

Source:
Guy 2024, pp. 13–14:
https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/156478/1/The%20distribution%20of%203.%20pl.%20pres.%20ind.%20-ynt%20ORCA%20VERSION.pdf

Implication:
At least some "early Myrddin" material demonstrably belongs to the post-Conquest prophetic environment, even when preserved only in substantially later manuscripts.

State: SURVIVES.

## Finding 6 — Prophetic texts are adaptive and attribution is fluid

The Welsh Merlin Poetry Project reports examples in which essentially the same prophetic poem is assigned to different speaker-pairs across manuscripts: Myrddin/Culfardd, Myrddin/Cwsg-fardd, Taliesin/Bardd Adda, and Taliesin/Myrddin.

Source:
https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/welsh/news/features/prosiect-barddoniaeth-myrddin-what-treasures-have-been-unearthed

Implication:
Prophetic-author identity can be a mobile feature of transmission. Attribution to "Myrddin" is therefore evidence for a manuscript/tradition's use of Myrddin as prophetic authority before it is evidence for authorship or biography.

State: SURVIVES.

## Finding 7 — Current scholarship describes Merlin as multi-source, not a clean biography

The 2026 Cambridge History chapter by Aled Llion Jones and Victoria Flood describes the Arthurian Merlin as developing through multiple and potentially multidirectional insular traditions, centring on two related figures first conflated by Geoffrey of Monmouth:
1. a northern wild man prophesying in the Caledonian Forest;
2. a child prophet associated with Carmarthen who interprets the red and white dragons.

Source:
https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-arthurian-literature-and-culture/early-merlin-and-early-myrddin/2B7AA8308A180AFA6591A12A4730D0FC

Implication:
"Merlin" is best treated as a convergence node in the textual tradition unless a narrower historical identity can be independently demonstrated.

State: SURVIVES.

## Finding 8 — Lailoken source itself marks Merlin identification as uncertain

The Latin `Vita Merlini Silvestris`, surviving in fifteenth-century BL Cotton Titus A XIX but possibly drawing on earlier Kentigern material, introduces a naked, hairy, mad wilderness figure named Lailoken and states that "certain people say" he was Merlin, while explicitly adding uncertainty.

The same narrative makes Lailoken claim responsibility for those killed in a famous battle between Liddel and Carwannok, describes a visionary rupture in which the sky opens and armed hosts appear, and gives him a prophetic reputation around Glasgow before his threefold death.

Edition/translation:
Winifred and John MacQueen, "Vita Merlini Silvestris," Scottish Studies.
https://open.journals.ed.ac.uk/ScottishStudies/article/download/596/622/651

Textual implication:
Even this witness does not simply say Lailoken = Merlin as uncontested fact. The equation was already a claim inside the transmission.

State: LAILOKEN/MERLIN IDENTITY = UNRESOLVED.

## Revised historical-core ceiling

### What survives strongly
- A real sixth-century northern British political milieu.
- Arfderydd as an annalistically preserved battle.
- A medieval Welsh forest-prophet tradition attached to Arfderydd/Gwenddolau/Rhydderch/Coed Celyddon material.
- A named Myrddin prophetic authority in medieval Welsh transmission.
- A distinct Lailoken northern wild-man tradition.
- Geoffrey's explicit literary fusion into Merlin.

### What remains unresolved
- whether the unnamed forest speaker was originally Myrddin;
- whether Myrddin was ever a sixth-century personal name;
- whether Lailoken and Myrddin preserve one historical person;
- whether any poem contains sixth-century composition;
- whether the wild-man story began as biography or folktale;
- whether Geoffrey inherited an already-fused Myrddin/wild-man figure or performed the decisive fusion himself.

## Hostile challenge

> The corpus can look ancient because it talks about sixth-century people and places, uses archaic-looking linguistic features, and survives in manuscripts containing genuinely old material. None of those facts independently proves sixth-century authorship. Prophetic texts demonstrably absorb later events, migrate between attributed speakers, and survive in recensions that differ substantially. A "historical Merlin" reconstructed by simply collecting the oldest-looking details from different witnesses may be an artifact created by modern source harmonization.

Status after challenge:
HISTORICAL MERLIN = UNRESOLVED.
EARLY MYRDDIN TRADITION = SURVIVES.
SINGLE-BIOGRAPHY MODEL = NOT_ADMITTED AS DEFAULT.
