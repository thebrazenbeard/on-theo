# Merlin / Myrddin Historical-Core Research Packet V1

Status: PROVISIONAL / SOURCE-BOUND
Date: 2026-10-03
Repository role: ON_THEO research
Claim type: PRIMARY TEXT + HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION + LATER TRADITION + SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION

## Research question

Does the Merlin complex preserve a historical person in the way that later Jesus traditions preserve a historical Yeshua, or is Merlin better understood as a literary fusion of multiple older figures, motifs, and historical settings?

## Evidence firewall

Preserve:
- HISTORICAL EVENT != HISTORICAL MERLIN
- EARLY LEGEND != CONTEMPORARY BIOGRAPHY
- LATER HAGIOGRAPHY != SIXTH-CENTURY EVIDENCE
- NAME ETYMOLOGY != PERSON
- SIMILARITY != IDENTITY
- ORAL-TRADITION CLAIM != DEMONSTRATED TRANSMISSION
- GEOFFREY'S MERLIN != EARLY MYRDDIN/LAILOKEN BY DEFAULT

## Claim 1 — Battle of Arfderydd is historically anchored

Evidence class: HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION / ANNALISTIC WITNESS

The oldest surviving witness of the Annales Cambriae, BL Harley 3859 (copied c. 1100), records only "Bellum Armterid" for the event conventionally dated 573 (or perhaps 575). A later thirteenth-century manuscript expands the notice to name the sons of Eliffer, Gwenddolau, and adds "Merlin went mad." The form "Merlinus" reflects Geoffrey of Monmouth and therefore cannot serve as independent sixth-century evidence for Merlin.

Sources:
- Andrew Breeze, *British Battles 493–937*, ch. 3, Cambridge/Anthem summary:
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/british-battles-493937/573-legends-of-merlin-at-arfderydd-or-arthuret-cumbria/C1EB5FB46F82422A6FC781AB30CACBB6
- *Annales Cambriae* edition overview:
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/annales-cambriae/FA9A5A9A5471EE9A19665CAAF0D997E6

Assessment:
- Arfderydd battle: STRONGER HISTORICAL CORE
- Merlin at Arfderydd: LATER ACCRETION / NOT CONTEMPORARILY ATTESTED

## Claim 2 — Earliest Welsh Myrddin material is much later than the alleged sixth-century life

Evidence class: MANUSCRIPT WITNESS / SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION

The Black Book of Carmarthen (Peniarth MS 1), copied around the mid-thirteenth century, preserves poems associated with the Myrddin legend: *Yr Afallennau*, *Yr Oianau*, and *Ymddiddan Myrddin a Thaliesin*. The National Library of Wales states that the first two contain later prophetic additions referring to Norman-era events but may preserve an older nucleus, probably ninth or tenth century, concerning a warrior who goes mad after Arfderydd and lives as a prophetic wild man in the Caledonian Forest.

Sources:
- National Library of Wales, Black Book of Carmarthen:
  https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/the-black-book-of-carmarthen
- National Library archival description, Peniarth MS 1:
  https://archives.library.wales/index.php/llyfr-du-caerfyrddin
- Patrick Sims-Williams, *The Medieval Welsh Englynion y Beddau* (2023), dating discussion:
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/medieval-welsh-englynion-y-beddau/dating-englynion-y-beddau/369965E3F91367C6F949A38536717686

Assessment:
The poetry may preserve older material, but the surviving manuscript is ~650–700 years later than Arfderydd. Individual strata must be dated separately.

## Claim 3 — "Myrddin" may itself be a secondary literary name

Evidence class: SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION / ETYMOLOGY

A major scholarly line, associated especially with A. O. H. Jarman and revisited by Oliver Padel and later Merlin scholarship, argues that the prophetic name Myrddin may have developed through etiological speculation on the place-name Caerfyrddin (Carmarthen), whose ancient name derives from British *Moridūnon ("sea-fort/town"), rather than preserving a sixth-century personal name. On this model, the Welsh Myrddin persona later absorbs traits from a northern wild-man figure, Lailoken.

Sources:
- Nikolai Tolstoy, "Geoffrey of Monmouth and the Merlin Legend":
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/arthurian-literature-xxv/geoffrey-of-monmouth-and-the-merlin-legend/64BC22AF7E96575D3ED15531CBE98E68
- Aled Llion Jones & Victoria Flood, "Early Merlin and Early Myrddin" (2026):
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-arthurian-literature-and-culture/early-merlin-and-early-myrddin/2B7AA8308A180AFA6591A12A4730D0FC

Assessment:
HISTORICAL NAME "MYRDDIN": UNRESOLVED.
This materially weakens direct biography reconstruction.

## Claim 4 — Lailoken is a distinct northern wild-man/seer tradition

Evidence class: LATER TRADITION / HAGIOGRAPHY

Twelfth-century and later Kentigern material contains a northern wild man called Lailoken/Laloecen. In the *Vita Merlini Silvestris* tradition he is linked to Kentigern, prophecy, wilderness, and a threefold death. Jocelin of Furness's *Life of Kentigern* (c. 1180) preserves a related Lailoken story and explicitly acknowledges using earlier written and oral materials, but his work is late hagiography and must not be read as direct sixth-century biography.

Sources:
- Jocelin, *Life of Kentigern*, translation/source discussion:
  https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/Jocelyn-LifeofKentigern.asp
- Cynthia Whidden Green, contextual study and translation:
  https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/CynthiaWhiddenGreen-SaintKentigern1998.asp
- Helen Birkett, "Restoring the Text: Jocelin's Approach to the Vita S. Kentegerni":
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/saints-lives-of-jocelin-of-furness/restoring-the-text-jocelins-approach-to-the-vita-s-kentegerni/36309B4519AAB2AF6613EAA8355E3C53

Assessment:
LAILOKEN TRADITION: SECURE AS MEDIEVAL NORTHERN TRADITION.
SIXTH-CENTURY HISTORICAL LAILOKEN: UNRESOLVED.

## Claim 5 — The "mad survivor becomes seer" motif is not unique to Merlin

Evidence class: COMPARATIVE LITERATURE / FOLKLORE

The Irish *Buile Suibhne* ("The Madness of Suibhne") presents a king who goes mad in connection with the battle of Mag Rath (637), flees civilization, and lives a wild/bird-like existence. Scholars have long compared the Suibhne and Myrddin/Lailoken complexes. The threefold-death motif attached to Lailoken also belongs to a wider international folktale type.

Sources:
- Cambridge discussion of *Buile Suibhne*:
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/seamus-heaney-and-medieval-poetry/sweeney-astray/ECAFB571D2060010DC337531CB6584AB
- Cambridge, *Celtic Curses*, comparison with Merlin:
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/celtic-curses/incantations/F74829F077D73D4B41685E5F80B42A06
- JSTOR, "The Threefold Death in Early Irish Sources":
  https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvpj7bnr.14

Assessment:
The wild-man/madness/prophecy complex may be a reusable narrative type. This reduces the evidentiary value of motif similarity for reconstructing a unique historical individual.

## Claim 6 — Geoffrey of Monmouth explicitly fuses separate traditions

Evidence class: PRIMARY/LITERARY TEXT + SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION

The ninth-century *Historia Brittonum* gives the Vortigern dragon episode to a boy named Ambrosius/Embresguletic, not Merlin. Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Historia Regum Britanniae* (c. 1136–38) substitutes Merlinus Ambrosius, incorporates prophetic material, and places the resulting figure in the Vortigern/Arthurian political narrative. His later *Vita Merlini* (c. 1150) gives Merlin a different northern wild-man biography resembling the Myrddin/Lailoken complex.

Assessment:
GEOFFREY'S MERLIN is demonstrably composite.

## Claim 7 — Merlin's Arthurian role is secondary

Evidence class: SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION

A. O. H. Jarman and later scholarship emphasize that Myrddin and the Welsh prophetic tradition were not originally part of the Arthurian complex. Geoffrey's twelfth-century synthesis made Merlin central to Arthur's conception and reign.

Source:
- A. O. H. Jarman, "The Merlin Legend and the Welsh Tradition of Prophecy":
  https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.14491583.9
- Geoffrey scholarship:
  https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.14491531

Assessment:
"ARTHUR'S WIZARD MERLIN" = LATER LITERARY CONSTRUCTION.

## Historical-core models

### Model A — Single historical survivor
A real sixth-century Brittonic warrior/bard survived Arfderydd, underwent severe behavioral change, withdrew to the forest, and became remembered as a seer. Later names and stories accumulated around him.

State: POSSIBLE / NOT DEMONSTRATED.

### Model B — Historical Lailoken + secondary Myrddin
A northern historical or quasi-historical wild man called Lailoken stands behind the Arfderydd tradition; "Myrddin" develops independently in Wales and later absorbs Lailoken's biography.

State: PLAUSIBLE LITERARY-HISTORICAL MODEL / HISTORICAL PERSON STILL UNPROVEN.

### Model C — Pure legendary wild-man complex attached to a real battle
Arfderydd is historical, but the mad prophet is folkloric. A reusable Celtic/European wild-man and threefold-death complex becomes attached to a memorable sixth-century battle, with later Welsh and Scottish traditions producing Myrddin/Lailoken variants.

State: STRONG RIVAL EXPLANATION.

### Model D — Geoffrey-created Merlin from pre-existing fragments
The recognizable Merlin is principally Geoffrey's twelfth-century synthesis of Ambrosius, Welsh prophecy, northern wild-man material, and his own literary invention.

State: STRONGLY SUPPORTED FOR THE MEDIEVAL "MERLIN" CHARACTER.

## Comparison with Yeshua

Yeshua and Merlin should not be placed at the same historical confidence.

Yeshua:
- first-century textual witnesses within decades of the proposed lifetime;
- multiple early Christian sources with dependence controls;
- external first-century/early-second-century discussion.

Merlin/Myrddin:
- no surviving contemporary or near-contemporary biography;
- earliest secure historical anchor is the battle, not the man;
- surviving Myrddin manuscripts are centuries later;
- later annalistic "Merlin went mad" line is itself post-Geoffrey;
- central medieval Merlin is demonstrably a fusion.

Conclusion:
The analogy is useful only at the level **possible historical kernel -> mythic/literary amplification**. The evidence floor for a historical Merlin is much lower.

## Hostile challenge

> We may be making the same reverse-engineering error we are trying to avoid elsewhere: stripping away obvious medieval magic and assuming the residue is historical. A real battle, genuine sixth-century rulers, an old poetic tradition, and geographically coherent northern settings can make a legend feel biographical without proving that its central wild man ever existed. The name Myrddin may itself be secondary, Lailoken is late-attested, and the defining motifs have close folkloric analogues. The minimum model requiring the fewest unsupported assumptions is therefore: real Arfderydd + later wild-man/seer tradition + Geoffrey's fusion.

Status after hostile challenge: HISTORICAL MERLIN = UNRESOLVED; GEOFFREY'S COMPOSITE MERLIN = SURVIVES.
