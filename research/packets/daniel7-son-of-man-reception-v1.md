## Research question

How should ON_THEO relate:

- Daniel 7's "one like a human being";
- the holy ones who receive the kingdom in the interpretation;
- later Jewish individual/heavenly/messianic readings;
- later Gospel "Son of Man" language;

without collapsing them into one timeless title?

Core rule:

`DANIEL_7_HUMANLIKE_FIGURE`
!=
`FIXED_TITLE_SON_OF_MAN`
!=
`ONE_UNIFORM_SECOND_TEMPLE_EXPECTATION`
!=
`EVERY_GOSPEL_SON_OF_MAN_USE`.

## 1. Daniel 7 first

Daniel 7:13-14 presents "one like a human being" coming with the clouds of heaven to the Ancient of Days and receiving dominion, glory, and kingship.

The interpreting section then says that the holy ones of the Most High receive and possess the kingdom.

That creates an interpretive relationship that must stay visible.

The humanlike figure can be read:
- corporately;
- as an individual representative of the holy ones;
- as an angelic/heavenly representative;
- or through other combinations.

The text itself is therefore a source of the later debate, not a license to erase it.

## 2. Description is not automatically a fixed title

The Aramaic expression in Daniel 7:13 is comparative/descriptive: "one like a human being / one like a son of man."

It should not be silently rewritten into:

> Daniel introduces a figure whose established title is "the Son of Man."

Later Jewish and Christian literature may develop or reuse Danielic humanlike-figure language, but that is reception history.

## 3. Collective and individual readings are both historically serious

Modern scholarship reflects a longstanding interpretive split.

One line reads the humanlike figure primarily as the faithful/holy people represented over against the beastly empires.

Another sees an individual heavenly, angelic, divine-agent, or representative figure.

Some interpretations deliberately combine the two: an individual heavenly representative can stand for the people who receive the kingdom.

V1 therefore records:

`EARLIEST_DANIEL_7_REFERENT = CONTESTED`.

This packet does not choose a theological winner.

## 4. Later Jewish individualization is real

Later Jewish apocalyptic texts can interpret Daniel's humanlike figure more individually.

Scholarship on the Similitudes/Parables of 1 Enoch and 4 Ezra 13 identifies important developments:

- the figure becomes individualized;
- the figure can be identified messianically;
- preexistence or heavenly status can become explicit;
- the figure can play a more active judgment/victory role than in Daniel 7 itself.

That is evidence for reception/development.

It is not proof that Daniel 7 originally encoded every later feature.

## 5. There was no safely uniform "Son of Man doctrine"

A major modern debate concerns whether first-century Judaism possessed a standard apocalyptic redeemer called "the Son of Man."

The safer project conclusion is narrower:

- Daniel 7 supplied powerful humanlike/heavenly-rule imagery;
- later Jewish texts independently or partly convergently developed that imagery;
- some later texts individualize and messianize the figure;
- the extent to which those interpretations formed a widespread common expectation remains uncertain.

Therefore:

`LATER_DANIELIC_INDIVIDUALIZATION`
does not imply
`UNIVERSAL_FIRST_CENTURY_SON_OF_MAN_DOCTRINE`.

## 6. Son of Man and Messiah overlap only where sources make them overlap

The previous Messiah packet establishes that "messiah/anointed" is itself a source-variable category.

Daniel 7:13 does not use the Hebrew/Aramaic messiah title for the humanlike figure.

Later texts can identify a Danielic humanlike figure with a messiah.

That is a reception move.

Therefore:

`DANIELIC_HUMANLIKE_FIGURE = MESSIAH`

must be recorded source by source, not assumed as a lexical identity.

## 7. The Similitudes dating/source problem remains visible

The Similitudes of Enoch are important to the first-century Son of Man debate, but their date and relation to other traditions have been heavily discussed.

They are not represented among the Aramaic Enoch manuscripts found at Qumran.

Absence at Qumran does not prove late composition, but it prevents the Qumran Enoch fragments from being treated as physical witnesses of the Similitudes.

V1 therefore uses the Similitudes as later Jewish reception evidence with dating caution, not as a physical Qumran witness.

## 8. 4 Ezra 13 is later reception, not a contemporary Yeshua source

4 Ezra 13 also presents an individualized human figure commonly analyzed in relation to Daniel 7.

Its post-70 CE setting makes it important evidence for Jewish Danielic reception after the destruction of the Temple.

It is not a pre-ministry documentary witness to what every Jew in Yeshua's lifetime expected.

## 9. Gospel Son of Man requires its own source analysis

The Gospels repeatedly use "Son of Man" language, including explicit Danielic cloud imagery.

But several separate questions remain:

- Which sayings most clearly evoke Daniel 7?
- Which sayings are generic/self-referential Semitic idiom?
- Which are passion/vindication formulations?
- Which reflect post-Easter community interpretation?
- Which, if any, can be attributed to the historical Yeshua?

This packet does not solve those questions.

It creates the semantic guard needed before a Gospel-specific packet is attempted.

## 10. Strongest current synthesis

The defensible sequence is:

1. Daniel 7 contains a humanlike figure receiving dominion in a vision whose interpretation gives the kingdom to the holy ones.
2. The relationship between figure and holy ones admits serious collective, representative, and individual/heavenly readings.
3. "One like a human being" is not automatically a fixed title.
4. Later Jewish apocalypses can individualize and messianize Danielic imagery.
5. Those later developments do not prove one universally shared first-century "Son of Man" doctrine.
6. Gospel usage must be analyzed passage by passage rather than inherited wholesale from a reconstructed background category.

## 11. Claim ceiling

This packet does not establish:

- the one original referent of Daniel 7:13;
- that Daniel 7 originally described Jesus/Yeshua;
- that "Son of Man" was a universally recognized messianic title;
- that the Similitudes predate, postdate, or depend on Gospel Son of Man traditions;
- that 4 Ezra depends on the Gospels;
- that every Gospel use derives directly from Daniel 7;
- that Yeshua used the expression as a self-designation.
