## 1. Canonical baseline

The canonical gospels consistently place Mary Magdalene near the passion/resurrection tradition, though the narratives differ.

Textual propositions recorded here:

- Luke 8:1-3 names Mary called Magdalene among women traveling with Jesus and the Twelve and identifies her as one from whom seven demons had gone out.
- Mark names Mary Magdalene among women at the crucifixion, observing the burial location, and returning to the tomb.
- Matthew places Mary Magdalene among the women at the crucifixion/burial and among the women encountering the risen Jesus.
- John 20 gives Mary Magdalene an especially prominent individual role: she comes to the tomb, encounters the risen Jesus, and reports to the disciples that she has seen the Lord.

These are high-confidence **textual occurrence** claims.

They do not by themselves settle:
- the exact historical sequence of resurrection experiences;
- whether each gospel preserves an independent witness stream;
- Mary's formal office or institutional rank in the historical movement.

## 2. The prostitute claim is not a canonical identification

The canonical gospels do not identify Mary Magdalene as a prostitute.

Luke identifies her with the seven-demons healing tradition. That is not the same proposition as prostitution.

The unnamed sinful woman in Luke 7 is not named Mary Magdalene in that narrative.

Mary of Bethany is a separately named figure in John.

Later Western reception combined these figures. That later combination must not be silently projected back into the canonical text.

## 3. Gospel of Mary: work identity versus physical witnesses

The Gospel of Mary must be modeled as a work distinct from the surviving physical witnesses.

Current checked witness control:

### P.Oxy. L 3525

Oxford Papyrology identifies P.Oxy. L 3525 as a Greek Gospel of Mary papyrus and dates the physical witness to the third century.

This is physical-witness evidence, not a third-century timestamp for the work's original composition.

### P.Ryl. III 463

The University of Manchester identifies Greek P 463 / P.Ryl. 3 463 as a Gospel of Mary fragment from Oxyrhynchus. Its catalog description explicitly says that some scholars have only **tentatively** identified the female disciple Mary as Mary Magdalene.

Recent scholarship treats P.Ryl. 463 together with P.Oxy. 3525 as third-century Greek evidence for circulation of the Gospel of Mary at Oxyrhynchus.

### Berlin Codex 8502

The Berlin Papyrus Database catalogs P. 8502 as a Coptic codex containing the Gospel of Mary and dates the manuscript to the fifth century.

The Staatliche Museen collection page dates associated Berlin Codex material to the fourth century / late fourth century.

V1 records that institutional catalog discrepancy rather than choosing one date silently.

The Berlin codex is incomplete: the beginning and a central block of the Gospel of Mary are missing. The most complete surviving version is therefore still fragmentary.

## 4. Mary identity inside the Gospel of Mary

The work is titled for "Mary", and the surviving literary Mary has a privileged revelatory/teaching role.

Identification with Mary Magdalene is common and has substantial literary support in scholarship, but it is not completely uncontested.

Relevant evidence tension:

- the Berlin museum describes the figure as Mary Magdalene;
- the Manchester catalog says some scholars identify the female disciple as Mary Magdalene only tentatively;
- scholarship has argued both for Magdalene identification and for setting that identification aside as an interpretive assumption.

Therefore:

`GOSPEL_OF_MARY_MARY = MARY_MAGDALENE`

is recorded as:

`COMMON_IDENTIFICATION / CONTESTED_NOT_TEXTUALLY_EXPLICIT_IN_V1`.

The packet does not use that identification as a hidden premise.

## 5. Mary, Peter, and authority

The surviving Gospel of Mary contains a literary dispute in which Mary's reported revelation is challenged and defended.

That supports:

`GOSPEL_OF_MARY_CONTAINS_AUTHORITY_CONFLICT_LITERARY_MOTIF`.

It does **not** automatically establish:

`HISTORICAL_PETER_AND_MARY_PERSONALLY_FOUGHT_FOR_SUCCESSION`.

Modern scholarship has used Mary/Peter literary tensions to study apostolic authority and gender. Other scholarship cautions that the Gospel of Mary's central issue may be the Savior's authority/reliability rather than a historical Mary-versus-Peter struggle.

V1 therefore distinguishes:

1. textual dispute — strong;
2. literary authority politics — supported scholarly interpretation;
3. direct historical succession conflict — unresolved.

## 6. Gregory the Great and the composite Mary

Modern historical scholarship identifies Gregory the Great's 591 preaching as a major Western consolidation point for the composite Mary:

- Mary Magdalene;
- Mary of Bethany;
- Luke's unnamed sinful woman.

Gregory's Homily 33 on Luke 7 belongs to the **reception-history** layer.

This supports the proposition that late-sixth-century Western interpretation fused figures whom the canonical texts themselves present separately.

It does not prove that Gregory invented every earlier association, nor does it by itself establish why the composite identity became culturally powerful.

## 7. Did the prostitute/sinner reception deliberately suppress Mary?

Separate the following propositions:

### A. Canonical prominence

Mary Magdalene is prominent in passion/tomb/resurrection narratives.

Status: `TEXTUAL_FACT`.

### B. Later authority construction

Some noncanonical texts give a Mary figure privileged revelation, teaching, or authority and stage conflict with male disciples.

Status: `TEXTUAL_AND_RECEPTION_FACT`, with identity/source-specific cautions.

### C. Western composite sinner reception

Western Christianity later fused Mary Magdalene with other gospel women, strongly associating her with penitential sexual-sinner imagery.

Status: `RECEPTION_HISTORY`.

### D. Deliberate coordinated suppression

The composite/prostitute image was deliberately created as a coordinated institutional strategy to neutralize Mary Magdalene as a rival apostolic authority.

Status: `NONE_ESTABLISHED / MOTIVE_UNRESOLVED`.

The later image could have authority effects without every stage having one coordinated motive.

## 8. Strongest current synthesis

The strongest source-controlled conclusion is narrower than either traditional or counter-traditional storytelling:

- Mary Magdalene has substantial canonical textual prominence at the passion/tomb/resurrection boundary.
- John 20 gives her a direct messenger role to the disciples.
- later Christian texts could use a Mary figure as a vehicle for privileged revelation and authority;
- the Gospel of Mary provides genuine literary evidence of disputed authority, but its Mary identification and historical-community reconstruction require caution;
- Western reception eventually created a composite Mary linking Magdalene, Mary of Bethany, and Luke's sinful woman;
- the canonical gospels do not identify Mary Magdalene as a prostitute;
- no checked evidence establishes one coordinated historical campaign that invented the sinner/prostitute image specifically to erase a historically documented Magdalene succession office.

That leaves a serious research question rather than a solved conspiracy:

> How did Mary Magdalene's early resurrection-witness prominence interact with later literary authority constructions and much later Western penitential reception?
